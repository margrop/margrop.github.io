41 Free Online Logic Puzzles: A Quick Guide to the puzzle-*.com Series

Published: 2026-10-18 · Views --
网站推荐 Productivity Tools 益智游戏 puzzle

A quick question: the last time you played Sudoku, was it in a newspaper, or in an app with more pop-up ads than puzzles?

If you enjoy games that rely on reasoning rather than luck, and you don’t want to install a pile of apps, this series may earn its own folder in your bookmarks. It is called puzzle-*.com: a family of sites with one domain per puzzle, from Sudoku, Nonograms and Minesweeper to Slitherlink, Nurikabe and Star Battle—41 in total, all free, all running in the browser.

I found it by accident while looking for a Pipes game, then clicked through the banners at the bottom of the page one by one and realized it is a remarkably complete universe of logic puzzles. This post sorts all 41 by mechanic, shows each one’s official thumbnail with a one-line rule, and suggests a path for beginners.

1. What it is: one puzzle, one domain

The naming is as literal as it gets: puzzle- plus the puzzle’s English name. puzzle-sudoku.com is Sudoku, puzzle-nonograms.com is Nonograms, puzzle-loop.com is Slitherlink, puzzle-star-battle.com is Star Battle. A few puzzles share a domain—Minesweeper and Mosaic both live on puzzle-minesweeper.com, and Binairo and Binairo+ on puzzle-binairo.com.

The sites are translated into many languages; swap the leading www for a language code (for example cn for Chinese) to switch.

At the bottom of every puzzle page there is a “more puzzles” wall listing all 41 games, each with a thumbnail. Here is a real screenshot of that wall, taken from the Chinese Sudoku page. The thumbnails in the tables below come from it too:

2. A look inside: layout and shared controls

Take Sudoku as an example. The left column has the puzzle name, translator credits, the rules, and links to every size and difficulty. The board and toolbar sit in the middle, with Done, Restart, Print, Share and New Puzzle buttons below.

Things I like about it:

  • Free, nothing to install: it runs in the browser and works with touch. There are official iOS and Android apps too, but the web version is good enough. There are some ads on the page.
  • Progress is saved: close the tab halfway through and the same puzzle picks up where you left off.
  • Rules on the page: every puzzle explains its rules, and most link to a video tutorial.
  • A full difficulty ladder: sizes from 5×5 to 20×20 or larger, and easy, normal and hard levels.
  • Daily, weekly and monthly specials: besides random boards there are fixed special puzzles, with a Hall of Fame ranking solve times.

Controls are consistent across the series: left-click fills or shades, right-click marks an × or a dot meaning “definitely not here”. Most shading puzzles let you click and drag across a row of cells. The toolbar has zoom, undo and redo.

3. All 41 puzzles at a glance, in six groups

41 sounds like a lot, but grouped by what you actually do on the grid there are only six kinds: fill in numbers, shade cells, draw lines, place objects, divide the grid, and two that fit nowhere else. The rules are condensed from each site’s official description; the site is authoritative on details.

3.1 🔢 Fill in numbers: the Sudoku family (7)

The easiest group to start with, since most people have played Sudoku. Fill each cell with 1 to N with no repeats per row or column, plus each puzzle’s extra constraint—sums, comparisons, or how many towers you can see.

Thumbnail Puzzle and rules
Sudoku
Fill a 9×9 grid with 1–9 so no digit repeats in any row, column or 3×3 box
Jigsaw Sudoku
Sudoku rules, but the boxes have irregular shapes
Killer Sudoku
Sudoku plus cages: digits in each dotted cage add up to its corner clue and do not repeat
Kakuro
Fill white cells with 1–9; a clue in a black cell is the sum of the run to its right or below, with no repeats in a run
Skyscrapers
Place towers of height 1–N with no repeats per row or column; edge clues say how many towers are visible from that side
Futoshiki
Fill 1–N with no repeats per row or column, satisfying the greater-than and less-than signs
Renzoku
Fill 1–N with no repeats; a dot between two cells means their numbers are consecutive, no dot means they are not

Killer Sudoku is my top pick for a next step: cage sums make you reason about combinations (“three different digits adding to 7 can only be 1, 2 and 4”), which is far more interesting than elimination alone.

3.2 ⬛ Shade cells (13)

The biggest group. Every cell is either black or white, and number clues tell you which. The rules look different, but three constraints keep coming back: black cells don’t touch, white cells stay connected, and no 2×2 block of one color. Learn these three and the rest of the group gets much easier.

Thumbnail Puzzle and rules
Hitori
Shade cells so no unshaded number repeats in a row or column; shaded cells never touch orthogonally and unshaded cells stay connected
Heyawake
Numbered regions hold that many black cells; black cells never touch orthogonally, white cells stay connected, and a straight run of white cells crosses at most two regions
Nurikabe
Each number is an island of that many white cells, one number per island; the black “stream” is connected and has no 2×2 blocks
Kurodoko
Each number counts the white cells visible from it in four directions, itself included; black cells never touch orthogonally and white cells stay connected
Norinori
Shade exactly 2 cells per region; every shaded cell is part of a 1×2 domino, and dominoes touch only diagonally
LITS
Place one L, I, T or S tetromino per region; identical shapes never share an edge, all shaded cells connect, and no 2×2 blocks
Tapa
Clues list the lengths of black runs among the 8 surrounding cells; all black cells connect, with no 2×2 blocks
Yin-Yang
Color every cell black or white so each color forms one connected group, with no 2×2 of the same color
Nonograms
Clues beside each row and column give the lengths of its black runs; shade them to reveal a picture
Mosaic
A Minesweeper variant: each number counts the black cells in the 3×3 area around it, itself included
Kakurasu
The 1st cell in a row is worth 1, the 2nd worth 2, and so on; shaded cells must sum to the clue at the end of each row and column
Binairo
Place black and white circles: equal counts per row and column, no three of a color in a line, and no two identical rows or columns
Binairo+
Binairo with signs (LinkedIn’s Tango): “=” means same color, “×” means opposite colors

Nonograms (also called Picross) are the most rewarding: the clues beside each row and column give the lengths of its black runs, and when you finish you get a pixel picture.

Nurikabe combines all three constraints: the black wall must connect with no 2×2, and each white island’s size must match its number. It looks impenetrable at first, but start from the 1s (always surrounded by wall) and it opens up quickly.

If you play LinkedIn’s daily Tango, you will recognize Binairo+: equal black and white per row and column, no three in a row, plus “=” and “×” signs.

3.3 ➰ Draw lines (7)

These puzzles have you draw between dots or cells, usually forming one loop with no crossings or branches. They share a key deduction: every point on a loop has either 0 or 2 lines, never 1 or 3.

Thumbnail Puzzle and rules
Slitherlink
Connect dots into a single loop with no crossings or branches; each number says how many of its four sides are used
Masyu
Draw a loop through every circle: go straight through white ones and turn next to them; turn on black ones and go straight on both sides
Shingoki
Like Masyu—straight through white, turn on black—and the number in a circle is the total length of its two straight arms
Pipes
Rotate the tiles so all pipes join into one network with no closed loops
Hashi
Build straight bridges between numbered islands, at most two per pair, never crossing; each island’s bridges match its number and all islands connect
Slant
Draw a diagonal in every cell; numbers at corners say how many diagonals meet there, and the lines may not form a loop
Stitches
Join every block to each neighboring block with one stitch across their border, never sharing a hole; edge clues count the holes per row and column

Slitherlink is the best entry point: each number says how many of its four sides are drawn. Cross out everything around a 0, start from a 3 in a corner, and the board starts to unfold.

Masyu has only black and white circles, but the rules are clever: go straight through white circles and turn next to them; turn on black circles and go straight on both sides.

3.4 📍 Place objects (9)

Here you place things on the board—mines, bulbs, tents, ships, stars, lollipops—or fill aquariums and thermometers. Clues are usually counts: how many around here, how many in this row.

Thumbnail Puzzle and rules
Minesweeper
Classic Minesweeper: numbers count the mines in the 8 surrounding cells; find them all
Light Up
Place bulbs that light every white cell along rows and columns up to a black cell; bulbs may not light each other, and numbered black cells count adjacent bulbs
Tents
Pair each tree with one tent next to it orthogonally; tents never touch, even diagonally, and edge clues count the tents per row and column
Battleships
Find the fleet shown in the legend; ships never touch, even diagonally, and edge clues count occupied cells per row and column
Star Battle
Place 1 (or N) stars in every row, column and region, with no two stars touching even diagonally (LinkedIn’s Queens)
Lollipops
Complete lollipops—a circle plus a one-cell stick—so different lollipops never share an edge and same-type parts in a row or column are separated by another part
Aquarium
Fill aquariums with water at one level across each tank’s full width; edge clues count the filled cells per row and column
Thermometers
Fill thermometers with mercury from the bulb with no gaps; edge clues count the filled cells per row and column
Shakashaka
Place black right triangles in white cells so the remaining white areas are all rectangles (upright or at 45°); numbered black cells count adjacent triangles

Everyone knows Minesweeper, but this one differs from the Windows classic: all the numbers are given up front, so there is no guessing—pure deduction finds every mine.

Star Battle is LinkedIn’s Queens: one star per row, column and region, with no two stars touching even diagonally. Small boards make a good warm-up; the 10×10 two-star version is a real workout.

3.5 ✂️ Divide the grid (3)

Cut the whole board into pieces that each meet a condition. Simple rules, good for short breaks.

Thumbnail Puzzle and rules
Shikaku
Cut the grid into rectangles, each holding exactly one number equal to its area
Galaxies
Split the grid into galaxies, each with exactly one dot and unchanged when rotated 180° around it
Dominosa
Pair the numbers into dominoes so every number pair appears exactly once

3.6 ♟️ Others (2)

Two that fit nowhere else: a chess endgame where every move must capture, and an English word search.

Thumbnail Puzzle and rules
Chess Puzzles
Chess pieces move normally, but every move must capture (kings included) until one piece is left
Word search games
Boggle: chain adjacent letters into as many words as you can; longer words score more

4. A beginner path in five stops

Puzzles with related rules are easiest to learn together. Go through these five stops, starting each at 5×5 or easy, and move on once you can solve two or three on your own:

  1. Sudoku → Killer Sudoku → Kakuro: start with familiar number placement, then add sums and digit combinations.
  2. Minesweeper → Mosaic → Nonograms: deduce black cells from numbers—and Nonograms reward you with a pixel picture.
  3. Hitori → Nurikabe → Heyawake: practice “black cells don’t touch” and “white cells stay connected”.
  4. Slitherlink → Masyu → Shingoki: loop puzzles, built on “every point has 0 or 2 lines”.
  5. Star Battle, Binairo+, LITS: puzzles that combine several constraints. If you play LinkedIn’s Queens and Tango, jump straight to bigger boards.

5. General solving tips

  • Certain moves first: every step should have a clear reason. If you catch yourself “just trying”, there is a clue you haven’t used.
  • Use right-click marks: put an × on cells you’ve ruled out. Breakthroughs often come from what you eliminate, not what you fill.
  • Start from extreme clues: a 1 in Nurikabe, a 0 or 3 in Slitherlink, a 1 or N in Skyscrapers all give away a lot at once.
  • Stuck? Watch the tutorial: Yin-Yang and LITS rely on a few lemmas covered in the official videos; without them even easy boards feel hard.
  • Don’t chase the clock: the Hall of Fame is fierce; the fun is solving it yourself.

6. Q&A

Q: Do I need an account?

No. Just open a puzzle and play; progress is stored in your browser. There is an account entry in the top-left corner if you want to sync progress across devices.

Q: Does it work on phones?

Yes, touch is supported. Large boards get cramped on a phone, though—use a tablet or computer for 15×15 and up.

Q: Is it good for kids?

Very. No math knowledge is needed, just patience and logic. Start with 5×5 Minesweeper, Nonograms or Tents.

7. Final thoughts

The charm of logic puzzles is that every board has exactly one solution, and you can reach it without guessing. Starting from an empty grid and ruling things out one step at a time until the Done button lights up gives a feeling of everything clicking into place that few games match.

Pick one or two of the 41 you like and solve one a day—it’s great exercise for the brain. The series is also in my favorites for quick access.
